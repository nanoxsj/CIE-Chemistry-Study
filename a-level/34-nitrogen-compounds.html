34 Nitrogen compounds

Amines, phenylamine, azo compounds, amides and amino acids

34 Nitrogen compounds

本章对应 CIE 9701 2025–2027 syllabus 的 34.1 Primary and secondary amines、34.2 Phenylamine and azo compounds、34.3 Amides 与 34.4 Amino acids。题目部分对应专题题包 7.6 Nitrogen Compounds (A Level Only)；当前题包包含 Easy 与 Medium 两组 Structured Questions。

Learning objectives

学完本章后，你应该能够：

  • explain why aqueous amines are bases and compare the basicity of ammonia, aliphatic amines and phenylamine;
  • describe the preparation of primary and secondary amines from halogenoalkanes, and the reduction of nitriles and amides;
  • describe the preparation of phenylamine, diazonium salts and azo compounds;
  • write equations for the formation, hydrolysis and reduction of amides;
  • explain why amides are much weaker bases than amines;
  • describe zwitterions, isoelectric points, peptide bonds and the separation of amino acids by electrophoresis.

34.1 Primary and secondary amines

Structures and basicity

An amine contains a nitrogen atom with a lone pair. A primary amine has one carbon-containing group attached to nitrogen, while a secondary amine has two:

\[RNH₂ \qquad \text{primary amine}\]

\[R₂NH \qquad \text{secondary amine}\]

The lone pair makes amines Brønsted–Lowry bases: they accept a proton by forming a dative covalent bond to H⁺.

\[RNH₂ + H₂O ⇌ RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻\]

\[RNH₂ + HCl → RNH₃⁺Cl⁻\]

Alkyl groups have a positive inductive effect and push electron density towards nitrogen. This generally makes an aliphatic secondary amine more basic than ammonia. In phenylamine, the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available to accept a proton. A typical order is:

\[\text{phenylamine} < \text{ammonia} < \text{primary amine} < \text{secondary amine}\]

Worked example · Comparing amine basicity

Phenylamine, ammonia and an aliphatic secondary amine

Explain why phenylamine is a weaker base than ammonia, whereas an aliphatic secondary amine is a stronger base than ammonia.

  1. Base strength depends on the availability of the lone pair on nitrogen to accept H⁺.
  2. In phenylamine, the nitrogen lone pair overlaps with the benzene ring and is delocalised into the ring. It is therefore less available to accept a proton.
  3. Alkyl groups have a positive inductive effect and push electron density towards nitrogen.
  4. In an aliphatic secondary amine, two alkyl groups increase the electron density on nitrogen, making the lone pair more available.
  5. Therefore phenylamine is less basic than ammonia, while an aliphatic secondary amine is more basic than ammonia.

Preparation of primary and secondary amines

Halogenoalkanes react with ammonia by nucleophilic substitution. Use excess ethanolic ammonia, heat and pressure in a sealed tube. Excess ammonia reduces further reaction of the primary amine product:

\[CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br\]

A primary amine can also react with a halogenoalkane to give a secondary amine:

\[RBr + R'NH₂ → R–NHR' + HBr\]

Worked example · Preparing an amine

1-bromobutane to butylamine

State the reagents and conditions required for the conversion of 1-bromobutane into butylamine.

  1. Use excess ammonia, NH₃.
  2. Use ethanol as the solvent.
  3. Heat the mixture under pressure, usually in a sealed tube.
  4. The reaction is nucleophilic substitution, replacing bromine with NH₂.

Reduction of amides and nitriles

Amides are reduced to amines using lithium aluminium hydride, LiAlH₄, usually in dry ether followed by hydrolysis. An N-substituted amide gives a secondary amine:

\[RCONH₂ → RCH₂NH₂ + H₂O\]

\[RCONHR' → RCH₂NHR' + H₂O\]

Nitriles can be reduced to primary amines using LiAlH₄ in dry ether or H₂ with a nickel catalyst:

\[RCN + 2H₂ → RCH₂NH₂\]

Worked example · Reducing an amide

N-methylpropanamide

N-methylpropanamide can undergo reduction forming compound Y and water. State the reagent required and identify the type of amine formed.

  1. Use lithium aluminium hydride, LiAlH₄, in dry ether, followed by hydrolysis.
  2. The carbonyl group CONH is reduced to CH₂NH.
  3. Because the nitrogen remains attached to a methyl group and an alkyl chain, compound Y is a secondary amine.

Reaction with acyl chlorides

Ammonia and primary amines react with acyl chlorides by nucleophilic addition–elimination. The nitrogen lone pair attacks the electron-deficient carbonyl carbon, chloride is eliminated and the carbonyl group reforms. A second molecule of ammonia or amine neutralises the hydrogen chloride formed:

\[RCOCl + 2NH₃ → RCONH₂ + NH₄Cl\]

\[RCOCl + 2R'NH₂ → RCONHR' + R'NH₃Cl\]

For example:

\[CH₃COCl + 2CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + CH₃NH₃Cl\]

34.2 Phenylamine and azo compounds

Preparation and reactions of phenylamine

Phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂, is prepared from nitrobenzene by reduction. The syllabus method uses tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, with heat, followed by aqueous sodium hydroxide to liberate the free amine:

\[C₆H₅NO₂ → C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻ → C₆H₅NH₂\]

Hydrogen with a nickel, platinum or palladium catalyst, or LiAlH₄ in dry ether, are also suitable reducing systems.

Phenylamine reacts readily with bromine water because the NH₂ group activates the benzene ring and directs substitution to the 2-, 4- and 6-positions:

\[C₆H₅NH₂ + 3Br₂ → C₆H₂Br₃NH₂ + 3HBr\]

Worked example · Phenylamine synthesis

Nitrobenzene to phenylamine

Name the type of reaction and suggest suitable reagents and conditions for the conversion of nitrobenzene into phenylamine.

  1. The reaction is a reduction.
  2. Heat nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid.
  3. Add aqueous sodium hydroxide after the reduction to release phenylamine from its salt.
  4. An alternative is H₂ with a nickel catalyst, or LiAlH₄ in dry ether.

Diazonium salts and azo dyes

Phenylamine reacts with nitrous acid below 10 °C to form a diazonium salt. Nitrous acid is made in situ from sodium nitrite and dilute hydrochloric acid:

\[NaNO₂ + HCl → HNO₂ + NaCl\]

\[C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl + HNO₂ → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + 2H₂O\]

The diazonium ion contains the group –N≡N⁺ and is unstable at higher temperatures, so the reaction mixture is kept ice-cold or below 10 °C.

The diazonium ion acts as an electrophile and couples with a phenol or phenoxide ion. Coupling usually occurs at the position para to the hydroxyl group. The product contains the azo group, –N=N–, which extends conjugation between aromatic rings and gives intensely coloured azo dyes.

\[C₆H₅N₂⁺ + C₆H₅O⁻ → C₆H₅–N=N–C₆H₄–OH\]

Worked example · Stabilising a diazonium salt

Diazotisation and coupling

Phenylamine is converted into a diazonium salt before it is coupled with an alkaline phenol solution. State a condition that prevents the diazonium salt from decomposing and explain the role of the azo group in the dye.

  1. Keep the reaction mixture ice-cold, or below 10 °C.
  2. The low temperature prevents the unstable diazonium salt from decomposing.
  3. The diazonium ion is an electrophile and reacts with the electron-rich phenoxide ring.
  4. The product contains –N=N–, the azo group, which connects two aromatic systems.
  5. The extended delocalised system absorbs visible light, so the azo compound is strongly coloured.

34.3 Amides

Formation and hydrolysis

Amides contain the functional group –CONH₂ or a substituted form such as –CONHR. They are formed when ammonia or a primary amine reacts with an acyl chloride:

\[RCOCl + 2NH₃ → RCONH₂ + NH₄Cl\]

\[RCOCl + 2R'NH₂ → RCONHR' + R'NH₃Cl\]

Amides are hydrolysed by aqueous acid or alkali on heating. Acid hydrolysis gives a carboxylic acid and an ammonium ion; alkaline hydrolysis gives a carboxylate salt and ammonia:

\[RCONH₂ + H₂O + H⁺ → RCOOH + NH₄⁺\]

\[RCONH₂ + NaOH → RCOONa + NH₃\]

For example, heating ethanamide with sodium hydroxide produces sodium ethanoate and ammonia. The ammonia turns damp red litmus paper blue, providing a test for an amide.

Worked example · Testing for an amide

Ethanamide and sodium hydroxide

Ethanamide is heated with sodium hydroxide. Write the equation and explain how this reaction can be used as a test for an amide.

  1. The equation is:

    \[CH₃CONH₂ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + NH₃\]

  2. Heat the unknown amide with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

  3. Test the gas produced with damp red litmus paper.

  4. Ammonia is released and turns damp red litmus paper blue.

Reduction and weak basicity of amides

Amides can be reduced with LiAlH₄ to give amines:

\[RCONH₂ → RCH₂NH₂ + H₂O\]

Amides are much weaker bases than amines. The nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl group. It is therefore less available to accept a proton. The electronegative oxygen atom also withdraws electron density from nitrogen.

Worked example · Comparing amide and amine basicity

Benzamide and benzylamine

State which is the weaker base, benzamide or benzylamine, and explain your answer. State a suitable reducing agent for converting benzamide into benzylamine.

  1. Benzamide is the weaker base.
  2. In benzamide, the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl group.
  3. The carbonyl oxygen withdraws electron density, so the lone pair is less available to accept a proton.
  4. Use lithium aluminium hydride, LiAlH₄, in dry ether.

34.4 Amino acids

General structure, acid–base behaviour and zwitterions

An α-amino acid has the general formula RCH(NH₂)COOH. It contains both an acidic carboxylic acid group and a basic amino group.

In the solid state and in aqueous solution near neutral pH, the proton can transfer internally from –COOH to –NH₂. The resulting dipolar ion is a zwitterion:

\[RCH(NH₃⁺)COO⁻\]

In acidic solution the amino group is protonated, RCH(NH₃⁺)COOH. In alkaline solution the carboxyl group is deprotonated, RCH(NH₂)COO⁻.

Worked example · Amino acid acid–base reactions

Alanine in acidic and basic conditions

Draw the structures of alanine in acidic and basic conditions. Write equations showing the reactions of alanine with hydrochloric acid and serine with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

  1. In acidic conditions, alanine is protonated at nitrogen: CH₃CH(NH₃⁺)COOH.

  2. In basic conditions, alanine is deprotonated at the carboxyl group: CH₃CH(NH₂)COO⁻.

  3. With hydrochloric acid:

    \[CH₃CH(NH₂)COOH + HCl → CH₃CH(NH₃⁺)COOH + Cl⁻\]

  4. With sodium hydroxide, serine reacts as:

    \[NH₂CH(CH₂OH)COOH + NaOH → NH₂CH(CH₂OH)COONa + H₂O\]

Isoelectric point and electrophoresis

The isoelectric point, pI, is the pH at which an amino acid has zero net charge. At this pH it is present mainly as a zwitterion and does not move in an electric field.

In electrophoresis, a mixture is placed on buffer-soaked paper between two electrodes. Positive ions move towards the negative electrode, and negative ions move towards the positive electrode. The distance travelled depends on charge and size: highly charged and small ions generally move further and faster than weakly charged and large ions.

Worked example · Interpreting electrophoresis

Charge and relative size

An amino acid moves towards the positive electrode during electrophoresis. State the charge on the species. Explain why another amino acid may remain stationary.

  1. A species moving towards the positive electrode must be negatively charged.
  2. It is attracted to the positive electrode because opposite charges attract.
  3. An amino acid may remain stationary if it has zero net charge.
  4. This occurs when the buffer pH equals the amino acid’s isoelectric point; the amino acid is present as a zwitterion and has no net movement.

Peptide bonds and proteins

Two amino acids form a dipeptide by a condensation reaction between the amino group of one molecule and the carboxyl group of another. Water is eliminated and a peptide bond, –CO–NH–, forms:

\[H₂N–CHR–COOH + H₂N–CHR'–COOH → H₂N–CHR–CO–NH–CHR'–COOH + H₂O\]

A tripeptide contains three amino acid residues and two peptide bonds. Proteins are condensation polymers, also called polyamides, made from many amino acid residues.

Worked example · Counting amino acid residues

Silk protein

A silk protein has Mᵣ about 600 000 and is made from equal amounts of glycine, alanine and serine. Given Mᵣ values 75, 89 and 105, calculate the average number of amino acid residues.

  1. Each amino acid loses H₂O when a peptide bond forms, so subtract 18 from each Mᵣ:
    • glycine residue: 75 − 18 = 57
    • alanine residue: 89 − 18 = 71
    • serine residue: 105 − 18 = 87
  2. One set containing one residue of each amino acid has Mᵣ = 57 + 71 + 87 = 215.
  3. Number of sets = 600 000 ÷ 215 = 2791.
  4. Each set contains three residues, so total residues = 2791 × 3 = 8372.

Example 4 · Basicity, phenylamine and azo compounds · 7.6 SQ Medium Q1

Three nitrogen-containing molecules, ammonia, NH₃, phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂ and N-methylethylamine, CH₃CH₂NHCH₃, are drawn below respectively in Fig. 1.1.

(a)(i) List the three amine molecules drawn in Fig. 1.1 in order of increasing base strength. [1 mark]

(a)(ii) Explain your answer to part (a)(i). [6 marks]

(b) Phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂, shown in Fig. 1.1 can be produced from nitrobenzene, C₆H₅NO₂. Name the type of reaction and suggest suitable reagents and conditions for this conversion. [3 marks]

(c) Azo dyes are organic compounds, which are largely used in the treatment of textiles and leather products, as well as in food. Phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂, can be used in the manufacture of azo dyes. The manufacturing process is outlined below.

Step 1: Phenylamine is dissolved in HCl to produce a diazonium salt. A diazonium salt contains two nitrogen atoms joined together by a triple bond. The reaction for this process is:

C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl + HNO₂ → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + 2H₂O

Step 2: This solution is then slowly added to an alkaline solution of a phenol coupling agent to form the dye.

(c)(i) The diazonium salt, C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻, is an unstable compound. Suggest a condition that could be added to ensure that the salt would not break down during the reaction. [1 mark]

(c)(ii) Draw the structure of the diazonium salt formed in Step 1, showing the displayed formula of the nitrogen-containing group. [1 mark]

(d) Phenol, C₆H₅OH, is an aromatic organic compound which is also crucial in the manufacture of azo dyes. The final step of the azo dye production involves pairing up the diazonium salt with a phenol compound as a coupling agent. Suggest a structure for the azo dye if the coupling agent used is 2,6-dimethylphenol. [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a)(i) phenylamine < ammonia < N-methylethylamine. [1]
  • (a)(ii) Base strength depends on the availability of the lone pair on nitrogen. The two alkyl groups in N-methylethylamine have a positive inductive effect and push electron density towards nitrogen, so its lone pair is most available. In phenylamine, the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring and is less available to accept H⁺. [6]
  • (b) The reaction is a reduction. Suitable methods include tin/concentrated HCl/heat followed by aqueous NaOH; H₂ with a Ni/Pt/Pd catalyst; or LiAlH₄ in dry ether. [3]
  • (c)(i) Keep the mixture at low temperature, in ice-cold conditions or below 10 °C. [1]
  • (c)(ii) C₆H₅–N≡N⁺ Cl⁻, with the positive charge on the correct nitrogen. [1]
  • (d) Couple the diazonium ion to the position para to the OH group of 2,6-dimethylphenol. A correct product contains C₆H₅–N=N–C₆H₂(CH₃)₂OH, with the OH group opposite the azo link. [2]

Example 5 · Acyl chlorides and amines · 7.6 SQ Medium Q2

Amines will react with acid chlorides to produce a different nitrogen-containing product.

(a)(i) Name and outline the mechanism between ethanoyl chloride, CH₃COCl, and methylamine, CH₃NH₂. [5 marks]

(a)(ii) Give the IUPAC name of the compound formed in the reaction. [1 mark]

(b)(i) Methylamine can be prepared from chloromethane. Give the other reagent, and any reaction conditions necessary for the preparation of methylamine. [3 marks]

(b)(ii) Give the name of the mechanism between chloromethane and the other reagent to form methylamine. [1 mark]

(c)(i) Chloroethane can also react with methylamine. Draw the displayed formula of the organic compound formed in this reaction. [1 mark]

(c)(ii) Give the classification of this compound. [1 mark]

(d)(i) Two separate solutions of methylamine, CH₃NH₂, and dimethylamine, (CH₃)₂NH, were left unlabelled in a laboratory. Suggest how, other than smell, they could be distinguished from one another. [2 marks]

(d)(ii) Give an explanation to your answer to part (d)(i). [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a)(i) The mechanism is nucleophilic addition–elimination: the nitrogen lone pair attacks the δ⁺ carbonyl carbon; the C=O bond opens to give a tetrahedral intermediate; the C=O bond reforms and Cl⁻ leaves; finally a proton is removed from nitrogen. The intermediate has N⁺ and O⁻. [5]
  • (a)(ii) The product is N-methylethanamide. [1]
  • (b)(i) Use ammonia, NH₃; excess hot ethanolic ammonia; and pressure. [3]
  • (b)(ii) Nucleophilic substitution. [1]
  • (c)(i) CH₃CH₂–NH–CH₃. [1]
  • (c)(ii) It is a secondary amine. [1]
  • (d)(i) Use a pH meter. Dimethylamine gives the higher pH reading. [2]
  • (d)(ii) Two alkyl groups in dimethylamine have a positive inductive/electron-donating effect, making its lone pair more available than in methylamine. [2]

(b) Amino acids can act as acids or bases. Write equations to show (i) the reaction between alanine and HCl(aq), and (ii) the reaction between serine and NaOH(aq). [2 marks]

(c)(i) Draw the structural formula of a portion of the silk protein, showing three main acid residues. Label a peptide bond on your structure. [3 marks]

(c)(ii) State the polymer type of silk protein. [1 mark]

(d) The Mᵣ of a silk protein molecule is about 600 000. Assuming it is made from equal amounts of the three amino acids in Fig. 3.1, calculate the average number of amino acid residues in the protein chain. Show your working. [Mᵣ(glycine) = 75; Mᵣ(alanine) = 89; Mᵣ(serine) = 105] [3 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) ⁺NH₃CH₂COO⁻. [1]
  • (b)
    • NH₂CH(CH₃)CO₂H + HCl → Cl⁻ NH₃⁺CH(CH₃)CO₂H. [1]
    • NH₂CH(CH₂OH)CO₂H + NaOH → NH₂CH(CH₂OH)CO₂Na + H₂O. [1]
  • (c)(i) One acceptable portion is –NH–CH(CH₃)–CO–NH–CH(CH₂OH)–CO–NH–CH₂–CO–. It contains three glycine/alanine/serine residues, two C(O)–NH peptide bonds, and at least one labelled peptide bond. [3]
  • (c)(ii) Silk is a condensation polymer, or polyamide. [1]

Phenylamine can be converted into the organic compounds A and B as shown in Fig. 4.1.

(a)(i) Suggest the structural formulae of A and B in the boxes in Fig. 4.1. [2 marks]

(i) Name the functional group formed in this reaction. [1 mark]

  • (d)(i) The organic product is benzoic acid, C₆H₅COOH. [1]
