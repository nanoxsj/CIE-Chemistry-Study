3 Chemical bonding

Electronegativity, bonding, shapes and intermolecular forces

3 Chemical bonding

本章对应 CIE 9701 AS Chemistry 的 Chemical bonding。内容覆盖 electronegativity、ionic bonding、metallic bonding、covalent and coordinate bonding、shapes of molecules、intermolecular forces、bond properties 以及 dot-and-cross diagrams。

Learning objectives

学完本章后，你应该能够：

  • define electronegativity and explain trends across a period and down a group;
  • describe ionic, metallic, covalent and coordinate bonding;
  • explain how bonding and structure determine physical properties;
  • draw dot-and-cross diagrams for atoms, ions, molecules and polyatomic ions;
  • use electron-pair repulsion to predict molecular shapes and bond angles;
  • distinguish sigma and pi bonds;
  • explain London dispersion forces, permanent dipole forces and hydrogen bonding;
  • relate bond polarity, bond length and bond energy to electronegativity and atomic size.

3.1 Electronegativity and bond polarity

Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

Across a period, electronegativity generally increases because nuclear charge increases while the bonding electrons remain in the same principal shell. Atomic radius decreases and the attraction for the bonding pair becomes stronger.

Down a group, electronegativity generally decreases because the bonding pair is further from the nucleus and more inner shells shield the nuclear charge.

Fluorine is the most electronegative element in the usual CIE scale. The noble gases are normally omitted because they do not commonly form covalent bonds in the AS syllabus.

Bond polarity and dipoles

If two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the bonding electrons are attracted more strongly to one atom. The more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge, \(\delta-\), and the other atom carries a partial positive charge, \(\delta+\).

The C–H bond is treated as approximately non-polar at this level. C–O, C–F and C–Cl bonds are polar with the carbon atom as \(\delta+\) and the more electronegative atom as \(\delta-\).

Bond polarity is not the same as molecular polarity. A molecule can contain polar bonds but have no overall dipole if the bond dipoles cancel because of its symmetrical shape, as in \(\ce{CO2}\).

3.2 Ionic bonding

Formation of ions and ionic lattices

An ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. A metal atom forms a positive ion by losing one or more outer-shell electrons; a non-metal atom forms a negative ion by gaining electrons.

The ions arrange themselves in a giant three-dimensional lattice. Each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions, so the attraction acts in all directions. The formula of an ionic compound gives the simplest whole-number ratio of ions that makes the compound electrically neutral.

Examples:

Ions Formula
\(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) \(\ce{NaCl}\)
\(\ce{Mg^{2+}}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) \(\ce{MgCl2}\)
\(\ce{Al^{3+}}\) and \(\ce{O^{2-}}\) \(\ce{Al2O3}\)
\(\ce{Ca^{2+}}\) and \(\ce{PO4^{3-}}\) \(\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}\)

Properties of ionic compounds

Ionic compounds usually have high melting and boiling points because many strong electrostatic attractions must be overcome.

They do not conduct electricity when solid because the ions are fixed in position. They conduct when molten or dissolved in water because the ions are then mobile.

An ionic crystal is brittle. When a layer shifts, ions of the same charge can become adjacent and repel strongly, causing the crystal to split.

3.3 Metallic bonding

Metallic structure

Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons. The outer electrons are not associated with one particular atom and can move through the structure.

Metals conduct electricity because delocalised electrons carry charge. They conduct heat because mobile electrons transfer energy rapidly through the lattice.

Metallic bonding is non-directional. Layers of metal ions can slide while the attraction to the delocalised electrons remains, so metals are malleable and ductile.

Alloys

An alloy contains atoms of different elements. The different-sized atoms distort the layers of the metal lattice and make it more difficult for the layers to slide. Alloys are therefore usually harder than pure metals.

Increasing the number of delocalised electrons or increasing the charge density of the metal ions generally strengthens metallic bonding, although the exact properties depend on the alloy structure.

3.4 Covalent and coordinate bonding

Covalent bonding

A covalent bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the bonded atoms. A single covalent bond contains one shared pair; a double bond contains two shared pairs and a triple bond contains three shared pairs.

The shared pair is formed by overlap of atomic orbitals. A sigma (\(\sigma\)) bond is formed by end-on overlap along the line joining the nuclei. A pi (\(\pi\)) bond is formed by sideways overlap of parallel p orbitals and has electron density above and below the internuclear axis.

A double bond contains one \(\sigma\) bond and one \(\pi\) bond. A triple bond contains one \(\sigma\) bond and two \(\pi\) bonds.

Coordinate (dative covalent) bonding

A coordinate bond is a covalent bond in which both electrons in the shared pair come from the same atom. The donor must have a lone pair and the acceptor must have an empty orbital or be electron-deficient.

Examples include:

\[\ce{NH3 + H+ -> NH4+}\]

\[\ce{H2O + H+ -> H3O+}\]

The bond is represented by an arrow from the donor atom to the acceptor atom. Once formed, a coordinate bond behaves like an ordinary covalent bond.

Bond length and bond energy

A shorter covalent bond is generally stronger and has a greater bond energy. Between the same two elements, bond length decreases and bond energy increases in the order single bond < double bond < triple bond.

Down a group, larger atoms usually form longer, weaker bonds because the bonding pair is further from the nuclei. A large electronegativity difference increases bond polarity but does not by itself mean that a bond is fully ionic.

3.5 Shapes of molecules

Electron-pair repulsion

Electron pairs around a central atom repel one another and arrange themselves as far apart as possible. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs because their electron density is concentrated closer to the central atom.

The repulsion order is:

\[\text{lone pair--lone pair} > \text{lone pair--bond pair} > \text{bond pair--bond pair}\]

Multiple bonds are treated as one region of electron density, but they repel slightly more strongly than a single bond.

Electron regions around central atom Shape Typical angle
2 bonding pairs linear \(180^\circ\)
3 bonding pairs trigonal planar \(120^\circ\)
4 bonding pairs tetrahedral \(109.5^\circ\)
3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair trigonal pyramidal about \(107^\circ\)
2 bonding pairs + 2 lone pairs bent / V-shaped about \(104.5^\circ\)
5 bonding pairs trigonal bipyramidal \(90^\circ\) and \(120^\circ\)
6 bonding pairs octahedral \(90^\circ\)

Examples include \(\ce{CO2}\) linear, \(\ce{BF3}\) trigonal planar, \(\ce{CH4}\) tetrahedral, \(\ce{NH3}\) trigonal pyramidal and \(\ce{H2O}\) bent.

3.6 Intermolecular forces and bond properties

London dispersion forces

London dispersion forces arise from temporary fluctuations in electron density. A temporary dipole can induce a dipole in a neighbouring particle.

They act between all atoms and molecules. They become stronger when there are more electrons and a larger, more easily polarised electron cloud. Long molecules can also have stronger dispersion forces because they have a larger contact surface.

Permanent dipole forces

Polar molecules have permanent partial charges. The \(\delta+\) end of one molecule attracts the \(\delta-\) end of another. Permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces are stronger than dispersion forces for molecules of similar size.

The overall dipole depends on both bond polarity and molecular shape. The polar bonds in \(\ce{CO2}\) cancel, whereas the polar bonds in \(\ce{H2O}\) do not.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong permanent dipole attraction. It occurs when hydrogen is covalently bonded to N, O or F and is attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F in a neighbouring molecule.

Hydrogen bonding explains the relatively high boiling point of water and the high solubility of small alcohols and ammonia in water. It also contributes to the high surface tension of water.

Comparing physical properties

Boiling a molecular substance involves overcoming intermolecular forces, not breaking the covalent bonds inside each molecule. Larger molecules often have higher boiling points because their dispersion forces are stronger.

For molecules with similar size, a polar molecule generally has a higher boiling point than a non-polar molecule. Hydrogen bonding usually gives the largest increase among the intermolecular forces in this syllabus.

3.7 Dot-and-cross diagrams

A reliable drawing method

  1. Count the total valence electrons, adjusting for the charge of an ion.
  2. Choose a central atom; hydrogen is never central and the least electronegative suitable atom is usually central.
  3. Add single bonds and complete the outer atoms’ octets.
  4. Place remaining electrons on the central atom.
  5. If the central atom lacks an octet, form multiple bonds where appropriate.
  6. Put brackets around a polyatomic ion and write its charge outside the bracket.

In a dot-and-cross diagram, dots and crosses distinguish electrons contributed by different atoms. A shared pair must contain two electrons, while a lone pair remains on one atom.

Worked example · Ammonium ion

Formation of \(\ce{NH4+}\)

Ammonia has a lone pair on nitrogen. The hydrogen ion has no electrons. The lone pair is donated to \(\ce{H+}\) to form a coordinate bond, giving four N–H bonds and an overall \(1+\) charge.

The nitrogen atom has no lone pair remaining in \(\ce{NH4+}\), and the ion is drawn in square brackets with the charge outside.

Example 1 · Intermolecular forces · 1.3 SQ Easy Q1

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Easy Q1

(a) Name the three main types of intermolecular force and arrange them in increasing order of strength. [2 marks]

(b) Methanol, \(\ce{CH3OH}\), is a small polar molecule. Describe how two methanol molecules form a hydrogen bond. Include the partial charges and the lone pair involved. [3 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) London dispersion forces, permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces, hydrogen bonds, in that order.
  • (b) Oxygen is \(\delta-\) and the O–H hydrogen is \(\delta+\). The \(\delta+\) hydrogen is attracted to a lone pair on the oxygen atom of a neighbouring molecule. A hydrogen bond is shown as a dashed line.

Example 2 · Hydrogen bonding in water and ammonia · 1.3 SQ Easy Q2

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Easy Q2

Ammonia is very soluble in water.

(a) State why ammonia is polar. [2 marks]

(b) Describe two possible hydrogen bonds between one water molecule and one ammonia molecule. [4 marks]

(c) State one physical property of ammonia caused by its polarity. [1 mark]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) N–H bonds are polar and the molecule is trigonal pyramidal, so the bond dipoles do not cancel.
  • (b) A water \(\delta+\) H can hydrogen-bond to the lone pair on N; an ammonia \(\delta+\) H can hydrogen-bond to a lone pair on O. Partial charges and the dashed attractions must be shown correctly.
  • (c) It is soluble in water, or has a higher boiling point than a similar non-polar molecule.

Example 3 · Dot-and-cross diagrams · 1.3 SQ Easy Q3

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Easy Q3

Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for \(\ce{H2}\), \(\ce{Cl2}\) and \(\ce{HCl}\).

(a) Show the bonding pair and all lone pairs. [3 marks]

(b) Explain why the H–Cl bond is polar. [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) Each molecule must have one shared pair. \(\ce{H2}\) has no lone pairs; each chlorine in \(\ce{Cl2}\) has three lone pairs; chlorine in \(\ce{HCl}\) has three lone pairs.
  • (b) Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the bonding pair is closer to chlorine, giving \(\ce{H^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}}\).

Example 4 · Shapes and bond angles · 1.3 SQ Easy Q4

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Easy Q4

For each species, state the number of bonding pairs, number of lone pairs on the central atom, shape and approximate bond angle: \(\ce{CO2}\), \(\ce{BF3}\), \(\ce{CH4}\), \(\ce{NH3}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\). [10 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • \(\ce{CO2}\): 2 regions, 0 lone pairs, linear, \(180^\circ\).
  • \(\ce{BF3}\): 3 bonding pairs, 0 lone pairs, trigonal planar, \(120^\circ\).
  • \(\ce{CH4}\): 4 bonding pairs, 0 lone pairs, tetrahedral, \(109.5^\circ\).
  • \(\ce{NH3}\): 3 bonding pairs, 1 lone pair, trigonal pyramidal, about \(107^\circ\).
  • \(\ce{H2O}\): 2 bonding pairs, 2 lone pairs, bent, about \(104.5^\circ\).

Example 5 · Ionic bonding and physical properties · 1.3 SQ Easy Q5

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Easy Q5

Sodium chloride is a giant ionic lattice.

(a) Describe the structure of solid sodium chloride. [2 marks]

(b) Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity but molten sodium chloride does. [2 marks]

(c) Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point. [1 mark]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) A regular three-dimensional lattice of oppositely charged \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) ions; each ion is surrounded by ions of opposite charge.
  • (b) Solid ions are fixed, whereas molten ions are mobile and can carry charge.
  • (c) Strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions require much energy to overcome.

3 Chemical bonding - Structured Questions: Medium

Example 8 · Equilibrium and shape of \(\ce{PCl3}\) · 1.3 SQ Medium Q1

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Medium Q1

The equilibrium is:

\[\ce{PCl5(g) <=> PCl3(g) + Cl2(g)}\]

The yield of \(\ce{PCl3}\) increases as temperature increases.

(a) State whether the forward reaction is endothermic or exothermic and explain. [2 marks]

(b) State the shape and bond angle around phosphorus in \(\ce{PCl3}\). [2 marks]

(c) Explain why the bond angle is less than \(109.5^\circ\). [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) Endothermic; increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction and increases the product yield.
  • (b) Trigonal pyramidal; approximately \(107^\circ\).
  • (c) There are three bonding pairs and one lone pair. Lone-pair–bond-pair repulsion is stronger than bond-pair–bond-pair repulsion and compresses the angle.

Example 9 · Polarity and properties of ammonia · 1.3 SQ Medium Q2

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Medium Q2

(a) Explain the meaning of bond polarity using ammonia as an example. [2 marks]

(b) Explain why the ammonia molecule is polar. [1 mark]

(c) State one physical property of ammonia caused by its polarity. [1 mark]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) Nitrogen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the shared electron pair in each N–H bond is displaced towards N, giving partial charges.
  • (b) Ammonia is trigonal pyramidal, so the three N–H bond dipoles do not cancel.
  • (c) High solubility in water, or a relatively high boiling point compared with a similar non-polar molecule.

Example 10 · Bonding in ammonium chloride · 1.3 SQ Medium Q3

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Medium Q3

When ammonia gas reacts with hydrogen chloride, solid ammonium chloride forms.

(a) State the types of bond present within one formula unit of \(\ce{NH4Cl}\). [3 marks]

(b) State how many of each bond type are present if the coordinate bond is shown separately. [3 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) Ionic attraction between \(\ce{NH4+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\); covalent N–H bonds within ammonium; one of the N–H bonds is coordinate.
  • (b) One ionic attraction type, four N–H covalent bonds in the ammonium ion, with one formed originally as a coordinate bond. The coordinate bond is chemically equivalent to the other N–H bonds after formation.

Example 11 · Surface tension and hydrogen bonding · 1.3 SQ Medium Q4

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Medium Q4

A paperclip can float on water, but sinks after a small amount of soap is added.

Explain both observations in terms of intermolecular forces. [3 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer

Water has high surface tension caused by hydrogen bonding between water molecules. The surface can support the paperclip if it is placed carefully. Soap disrupts the hydrogen-bond network and lowers surface tension, so the paperclip sinks.

Example 12 · Comparing boiling points · 1.3 SQ Medium Q5

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Medium Q5

Compare the boiling points of \(\ce{F2}\) and \(\ce{HCl}\).

(a) Identify the intermolecular forces present in each substance. [2 marks]

(b) Predict which has the lower boiling point. [1 mark]

(c) Explain the difference. [3 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) Both have London dispersion forces; HCl also has permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces.
  • (b) \(\ce{F2}\) has the lower boiling point.
  • (c) HCl has stronger intermolecular attractions because it is polar, so more energy is required to separate its molecules.

Example 14 · Nitrate ion and resonance · 1.3 SQ Hard Q1

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Hard Q1

The nitrate ion is \(\ce{NO3-}\).

(a) Calculate the total number of valence electrons in one nitrate ion. [2 marks]

(b) Describe how the electrons are arranged in a valid dot-and-cross structure. [3 marks]

(c) Explain why the three N–O bonds are equivalent in the real ion. [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) N contributes 5, three O atoms contribute 18 and the negative charge contributes one: total 24 electrons.
  • (b) N is central, three O atoms are attached, the ion is placed in square brackets with a \(1-\) charge, and the octet/charge arrangement is shown correctly. Equivalent resonance structures can be drawn with the double bond in different positions.
  • (c) The actual ion is a resonance hybrid; the bonding electrons are delocalised over all three N–O bonds.

Example 15 · Methanamide shape and angles · 1.3 SQ Hard Q2

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Hard Q2

Methanamide has formula \(\ce{HCONH2}\).

(a) State the shape and approximate bond angle around the carbon atom. [2 marks]

(b) State the shape and approximate bond angle around the nitrogen atom. [2 marks]

(c) Explain why the two shapes are different. [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) The carbonyl carbon has three regions of electron density, so it is trigonal planar, about \(120^\circ\).
  • (b) Nitrogen has three bonding pairs and one lone pair, so it is trigonal pyramidal, about \(107^\circ\).
  • (c) The nitrogen lone pair gives four electron regions and stronger lone-pair repulsion; the carbon atom has three regions and no lone pair.

Example 16 · Coordinate bonds and expanded octets · 1.3 SQ Hard Q3

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Hard Q3

(a) Explain how \(\ce{PCl6-}\) can form from \(\ce{PCl5}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\). [3 marks]

(b) State the shape of \(\ce{PCl6-}\) and justify it using electron-pair repulsion. [2 marks]

(c) Explain why nitrogen cannot form \(\ce{NCl5}\). [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) A lone pair on \(\ce{Cl-}\) is donated to electron-deficient P, forming a coordinate P–Cl bond and the ion \(\ce{PCl6-}\).
  • (b) Octahedral; six bonding pairs repel equally and occupy positions as far apart as possible.
  • (c) Nitrogen is a Period 2 atom with no available d orbitals and cannot expand its octet beyond eight electrons.

Example 17 · Intermolecular forces between water and ammonia · 1.3 SQ Hard Q4

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Hard Q4

Water and ammonia can form hydrogen bonds with each other.

(a) Draw or describe the partial charges on \(\ce{H2O}\) and \(\ce{NH3}\). [2 marks]

(b) Describe both possible directions of hydrogen bonding between the two molecules. [4 marks]

(c) Explain why these interactions make ammonia highly soluble in water. [2 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) O and N are \(\delta-\); bonded H atoms are \(\delta+\).
  • (b) Water H can donate a hydrogen bond to the lone pair on N. Ammonia H can donate a hydrogen bond to a lone pair on O. Both attractions should be shown with dashed lines.
  • (c) Strong attractions form between ammonia and water molecules, replacing some water–water and ammonia–ammonia attractions and allowing the molecules to mix.

Example 18 · Solid \(\ce{PCl5}\) and liquid \(\ce{SbCl5}\) · 1.3 SQ Hard Q5

Original question page · 1.3 SQ Hard Q5

Solid phosphorus(V) chloride has a higher melting point than liquid antimony(V) chloride.

(a) State the particles and bonding in solid \(\ce{PCl5}\). [2 marks]

(b) State the particles and intermolecular forces in liquid \(\ce{SbCl5}\). [2 marks]

(c) Explain the difference in melting points. [3 marks]

Show mark scheme / model answer
  • (a) Solid \(\ce{PCl5}\) contains \(\ce{PCl4+}\) and \(\ce{PCl6-}\) ions in a giant ionic lattice with strong ionic attractions.
  • (b) Liquid \(\ce{SbCl5}\) contains covalent molecules held together by relatively weak intermolecular forces.
  • (c) More energy is needed to overcome strong ionic attractions in solid \(\ce{PCl5}\) than weak intermolecular forces between \(\ce{SbCl5}\) molecules.
